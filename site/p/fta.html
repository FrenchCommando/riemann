Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Euclid (key lemma); first complete proof Gauss, 1801
Theorem

Every integer n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 is a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique up to the order of the factors.

Existence is easy. Uniqueness is the real content — it fails in other number systems (in Z[−5]\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​], 6=2⋅3=(1+−5)(1−−5)6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})6=2⋅3=(1+−5​)(1−−5​)). The proof routes through Bézout's identity and Euclid's lemma.

    Every n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 is a product of primes.

    If nnn is prime, done. Otherwise n=abn = abn=ab with 1<a,b<n1 \lt a, b \lt n1<a,b<n; by strong induction aaa and bbb are products of primes, so nnn is too.

    More detail

    Strong induction lets us assume the result for every number below nnn, not just n−1n - 1n−1.

    • Base: n=2n = 2n=2 is prime.
    • Step: take n≥3n \ge 3n≥3 and assume every mmm with 2≤m<n2 \le m \lt n2≤m<n factors into primes. If nnn is prime, it's a product of one prime. If not, n=abn = abn=ab with both factors strictly between 1 and nnn. Each factors; concatenate the two lists.

Takeaway. Uniqueness is really Euclid's lemma, which is really Bézout. "Primes divide products only by dividing a factor" is the property that makes Z\mathbb{Z}Z special.
