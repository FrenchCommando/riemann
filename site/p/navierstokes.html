Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness

Millennium Prize problem, stated by Charles Fefferman, 2000
Theorem

Prove one of: smooth initial data in 3D always give a smooth solution for all time (A on R3\mathbb{R}^3R3, B on the torus, no external force), or some smooth data with a smooth force give a solution that breaks down in finite time (C on R3\mathbb{R}^3R3, D on the torus).

The proofs on the way here showed what viscosity does alone (the heat equation), the one global bound in 3D and why scaling defeats it (energy), why 2D flows stay smooth (vorticity can't grow), and what 3D adds (vortex stretching, and that any blow-up is a vorticity blow-up). This roadmap covers what has been proved since Leray, why the problem resisted, the 20-year search for a blow-up, and the 2026 claim that one has been found, with what is and isn't settled.

Roadmap, not a full proof. Steps marked cited state a major result with its source instead of proving it; the rest are worked out here.

  1. 1The question, preciselycited

    For smooth, divergence-free initial velocity u0u_0u0​ (decaying at infinity on R3\mathbb{R}^3R3, or periodic), does the solution of Navier–Stokes stay smooth, with finite energy, for all time?

    Fefferman, official problem description, Clay Mathematics Institute, 2000

    Smooth solutions exist for a short time; the question is whether they can stop being smooth. "Breakdown" means ∣u∣|u|∣u∣ becomes unbounded (equivalently its derivatives) at some finite time T∗T^*T∗. Alternatives C and D allow a smooth, suitably decaying force fff on the right side of the equation.

    More detail

    Why the force matters for the statement: with f=0f = 0f=0, any energy in the solution came from the initial data and only decreases. A force can keep pumping energy in, so blow-up with forcing is, in principle, easier to achieve. Clay nonetheless counts it: the official C and D allow a smooth force with the same decay as the data. Many analysts expect forcing to make little difference to whether blow-up is possible, but that expectation is not a proof.

  2. 2What has been provedcited

    Weak solutions exist for all time; smooth solutions exist for short times, and for all time if the data are small; in 2D everything is smooth forever; in 3D any singular set is very small.

    Leray 1934; Fujita–Kato 1964; Ladyzhenskaya 1959; Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg 1982

    Leray used the energy bound to build global "turbulent" (weak) solutions, but couldn't show they are smooth or unique. Fujita–Kato's fixed-point method needs smallness in a scale-invariant norm. In 2D energy is critical, which closes the argument. CKN: the singular set in space-time has one-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure zero.

    More detail

    The pattern is that everything that works uses either smallness, or a quantity that is critical or better under scaling. In 3D the only coercive global quantity, the energy, is supercritical, and every attempt to bootstrap from it to smoothness loses at small scales. That's the whole difficulty in one sentence, and the energy proof derives the scaling exponent.

    A related open question: are Leray's weak solutions unique? Buckmaster and Vicol (2019) showed that a still weaker notion of solution is badly non-unique, and Albritton, Brué and Colombo (2022) showed non-uniqueness of Leray solutions when a force is allowed.

  3. 3A barrier for regularity proofscited

    An "averaged" version of Navier–Stokes that keeps the energy identity and the standard harmonic-analysis estimates does blow up in finite time.

    Tao, J. Amer. Math. Soc. 2016

    Tao modified the nonlinearity so that it still does no work (energy is conserved exactly as before) but routes energy into ever smaller scales in a controlled cascade. Since the modified equation blows up, a regularity proof for the real equation must use some finer feature of the real nonlinearity.

    More detail

    The blow-up is engineered like a sequence of circuits, each transferring its energy to a copy of itself at a smaller scale, faster each time: exactly the scenario the scaling computation in the energy proof allows. Tao suggested that the real Navier–Stokes nonlinearity might be able to simulate such circuits, which would mean blow-up for the real equation too. That turned the search toward constructing a singularity.

  4. 4The search for a blow-upcited

    For the inviscid Euler equations (Navier–Stokes with ν=0\nu = 0ν=0), finite-time blow-up from smooth data was found numerically and then proved with computer assistance, in a domain with a boundary.

    Luo–Hou 2014 (numerics); Elgindi 2021 (rougher data); Chen–Hou 2022 (computer-assisted proof)

    Luo and Hou simulated an axisymmetric flow in a cylinder whose vorticity concentrates at a ring on the wall and appears to blow up. Elgindi proved blow-up for slightly non-smooth (C1,αC^{1,\alpha}C1,α) data. Chen and Hou proved blow-up near the Luo–Hou scenario for smooth data, using a computer-assisted stability analysis of an approximately self-similar profile.

    More detail

    Why Euler first: without viscosity there is no smoothing to fight. The strategy that emerged: find an (approximately) self-similar profile, a flow that reproduces itself at smaller scales and shorter times, and prove that nearby flows are attracted to it. Computer assistance enters through rigorous interval arithmetic bounds on the linearised stability problem. Moving from Euler to Navier–Stokes means showing viscosity can't stop the cascade. Scaling is what leaves room for that: under u↦λu(λx,λ2t)u \mapsto \lambda u(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t)u↦λu(λx,λ2t) the viscous term scales exactly like the nonlinear one, so viscosity does not automatically win at small scales.

  5. 5Status, November 2026cited

    On 8 September 2026 OpenAI announced a proof, formalised in Lean, that a smooth solution driven by a smooth external force can blow up in finite time, which addresses alternatives C and D. As of November it has not been independently verified, and the Clay Institute has not ruled.

    OpenAI, announcement of 8 Sep 2026; Clay Mathematics Institute statement; reporting listed below

    The announcement attributes the proof to a large group of AI agents running on an unreleased model, reaching the result in about 88 hours, followed by a Lean formalisation. The Clay Institute said the problem has "apparently been settled" but that its evaluation is unhurried; its rules require publication, two years and general acceptance.

    More detail

    What is reported, from the sources below:

    • Scope. The result uses a smooth force, so it is a C/D result. Critics, among them the mathematician Chris Budd, argue the problem "in its cleanest form", blow-up from the unforced equations, remains open, while noting that many expect forcing not to matter much.
    • Verification. Lean checks that the formal statement follows; whether the formal statement matches Clay's (boundary conditions, decay and regularity of the force) is exactly what human reviewers are now examining. The sources consulted here do not describe the blow-up mechanism in detail.
    • Priority. Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge reported their own breakthrough on the problem in August 2026 after about a year of work, and raised questions about whether it influenced OpenAI's models; OpenAI says the proofs differ significantly.

    As with the Riemann Hypothesis entry: this is taken from the reporting listed below, written with Claude, and the situation is changing.

  6. 6What would remaincited

    Even if the forced blow-up stands, the unforced question (can a smooth flow, left alone, develop a singularity?) stays open, along with uniqueness of Leray's solutions and what a singularity means for real turbulence.

    Fefferman 2000 (statement); Albritton–Brué–Colombo 2022 (forced non-uniqueness)

    With f=0f = 0f=0, any blow-up must be powered by the initial energy alone, which decreases. Turning a forced construction into an unforced one means making the flow generate its own "forcing" from a larger-scale part of itself, which is the kind of self-similar cascade the Euler work suggests might be possible.

    More detail

    Physically, a singularity in Navier–Stokes would mean the continuum model itself breaks down at small scales and must be replaced (by molecular dynamics, say) at least momentarily. Mathematically, it would explain why every regularity strategy failed: they were trying to prove something false. Either way, the Millennium problem as posed accepts a forced blow-up as a solution, so the prize question and the physics question have come apart.

Takeaway. Smoothness proofs need a quantity that beats scaling; the only global one, energy, loses. The search therefore turned to constructing a self-similar cascade, and in 2026 a forced blow-up was claimed.
