On the way to The Kakeya conjecture

Kakeya sets in the plane are two-dimensional

Antonio Córdoba, 1977 (this argument); Roy Davies, 1971 (Hausdorff dimension)
Theorem

Let KKK be a bounded set in the plane containing a unit segment in every direction, and 0<δ≤120 \lt \delta \le \tfrac120<δ≤21​. The points within δ\deltaδ of KKK form a set of area at least 12 (2+log⁡(1/δ)).\frac{1}{2\,(2 + \log(1/\delta))}.2(2+log(1/δ))1​. So covering KKK takes at least about δ−2/log⁡(1/δ)\delta^{-2}/\log(1/\delta)δ−2/log(1/δ) discs of radius δ\deltaδ: KKK has Minkowski dimension 222.

The previous proof made Kakeya sets of area zero. Area is too crude to see them, so look at them at a resolution δ\deltaδ: thicken the set by δ\deltaδ and measure that. A square stays at area about 111, a segment drops to about δ\deltaδ. This proof shows a Kakeya set in the plane behaves like the square, up to a factor log⁡(1/δ)\log(1/\delta)log(1/δ), so its dimension is 222. The argument is short: tubes in different directions overlap little, and Cauchy–Schwarz turns little overlap into a large union. It is the template for every approach to the higher-dimensional problem.

    More detail

    So δ\deltaδ-neighbourhoods of Kakeya sets in the plane can shrink, which the previous proof shows they do, but only at the rate 1/log⁡(1/δ)1/\log(1/\delta)1/log(1/δ): far too slowly to lower the dimension. Keich showed that rate is attained, so the logarithm can't be removed.

    Hausdorff dimension, which allows discs of different sizes in the same cover, is harder. Davies proved it is also 222 in 1971.

    In three dimensions the same argument gives only dimension 222: two tubes of width δ\deltaδ in space overlap in at most about δ3/sin⁡θ\delta^3/\sin\thetaδ3/sinθ, but there are about δ−2\delta^{-2}δ−2 directions, and the overlap sum is now too large. Pushing from 222 to 333 took until 2025. The destination of this path is that story.

Takeaway. Tubes in different directions overlap little, and Cauchy–Schwarz turns little overlap into a large union. So a Kakeya set in the plane, though it can have area zero, is two-dimensional.
