The Pythagorean theorem

Known in Babylon c. 1800 BC; proof in Euclid I.47
Theorem

In a right triangle with legs a,ba, ba,b and hypotenuse ccc:  a2+b2=c2\ a^2 + b^2 = c^2 a2+b2=c2.

There are hundreds of proofs. Here are three views of it: a picture proof that compares areas, a proof by similar triangles that comes down to two lines of algebra, and the vector identity that says what the theorem is really about.

Walk through it step by step a check at each step, detail adjusted to what you know
Proof · 8 steps
  1. 1Build a big square

    Make a square of side a+ba + ba+b and place four copies of the triangle in its corners, each with its legs along two sides of the square.

    Each side of the big square is split into a piece of length aaa and a piece of length bbb. The four hypotenuses then form a quadrilateral in the middle with all four sides equal to ccc.

    More detail

    Go around the big square: on each side, mark off aaa from one corner, leaving bbb. Put a copy of the triangle in each corner so that it uses the aaa-piece of one side and the bbb-piece of the next. The four triangles don't overlap, and their hypotenuses (each of length ccc) bound a region in the middle.

    So the big square is cut into four triangles plus one inner quadrilateral with sides c,c,c,cc, c, c, cc,c,c,c.

  2. 2The inner shape is a square

    Each corner of the inner quadrilateral is a right angle.

    At each corner, on the straight side of the big square, three angles meet: α\alphaα, β\betaβ (from two triangles) and the inner angle θ\thetaθ. They sum to 180∘180^\circ180∘, and α+β=90∘\alpha + \beta = 90^\circα+β=90∘, so θ=90∘\theta = 90^\circθ=90∘.

    More detail

    Call the triangle's acute angles α\alphaα (opposite aaa) and β\betaβ (opposite bbb). Since its angles sum to 180∘180^\circ180∘ and one is 90∘90^\circ90∘, we have α+β=90∘\alpha + \beta = 90^\circα+β=90∘.

    Each corner of the inner shape sits on a side of the big square. There, one triangle contributes α\alphaα and the neighbouring one contributes β\betaβ, and together with the inner angle θ\thetaθ they fill a straight angle: α+θ+β=180∘  ⇒  θ=90∘.\alpha + \theta + \beta = 180^\circ \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = 90^\circ.α+θ+β=180∘⇒θ=90∘. Four equal sides and four right angles: the inner shape is a square of side ccc, area c2c^2c2.

  3. 3Count the area two ways

    (a+b)2=c2+4⋅12ab(a + b)^2 = c^2 + 4 \cdot \tfrac12 ab(a+b)2=c2+4⋅21​ab.

    Left: the big square directly. Right: the same square as the inner square plus four triangles.

    More detail

    Area is additive, and the pieces exactly tile the big square with no overlap:

    • big square: (a+b)2(a+b)^2(a+b)2
    • inner square: c2c^2c2
    • each triangle: 12ab\frac12 ab21​ab, four of them: 2ab2ab2ab.

    So (a+b)2=c2+2ab(a+b)^2 = c^2 + 2ab(a+b)2=c2+2ab.

  4. 4Simplify

    a2+b2=c2a^2 + b^2 = c^2a2+b2=c2.

    Expand (a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2 and cancel 2ab2ab2ab from both sides.

    More detail

    a2+2ab+b2=c2+2ab  ⟹  a2+b2=c2.■a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = c^2 + 2ab \;\Longrightarrow\; a^2 + b^2 = c^2. \qquad \blacksquarea2+2ab+b2=c2+2ab⟹a2+b2=c2.■

    The 2ab2ab2ab — the four triangles — appears on both sides and cancels. That is the picture above: remove the four triangles from the big square and what remains is c2c^2c2; rearrange the same four triangles into two a×ba \times ba×b rectangles instead, and what remains is a2+b2a^2 + b^2a2+b2.

  5. 5Second proof: drop the altitude

    The altitude from the right angle splits the hypotenuse into q+p=cq + p = cq+p=c and cuts the triangle into two smaller right triangles, both similar to the original.

    Each small triangle has a right angle at HHH and shares one acute angle with the original, so all three angles match.

    More detail

    Label the triangle ABCABCABC with the right angle at CCC, so a=BCa = BCa=BC, b=ACb = ACb=AC, c=ABc = ABc=AB, and let HHH be the foot of the altitude from CCC. Then q=AHq = AHq=AH and p=HBp = HBp=HB, with q+p=cq + p = cq+p=c.

    • Triangle HBCHBCHBC has a right angle at HHH and shares the angle at BBB with ABCABCABC.
    • Triangle AHCAHCAHC has a right angle at HHH and shares the angle at AAA with ABCABCABC.

    Two equal angles force the third (the angle sum is 180∘180^\circ180∘), so both small triangles are similar to ABCABCABC.

  6. 6Now it is just algebra

    a2=cpa^2 = cpa2=cp and b2=cqb^2 = cqb2=cq, so a2+b2=c(p+q)=c2a^2 + b^2 = c(p + q) = c^2a2+b2=c(p+q)=c2.

    From HBC∼ABCHBC \sim ABCHBC∼ABC, comparing the side at BBB with the hypotenuse: pa=ac\frac{p}{a} = \frac{a}{c}ap​=ca​. Likewise qb=bc\frac{q}{b} = \frac{b}{c}bq​=cb​ from AHC∼ABCAHC \sim ABCAHC∼ABC. Cross-multiply and add.

    More detail

    Similar triangles have equal ratios of corresponding sides. Match the sides by the angle they touch:

    • In HBCHBCHBC the side at BBB is ppp and the hypotenuse is aaa; in ABCABCABC they are aaa and ccc. So pa=ac\frac{p}{a} = \frac{a}{c}ap​=ca​, i.e. a2=cpa^2 = cpa2=cp.
    • In AHCAHCAHC the side at AAA is qqq and the hypotenuse is bbb; in ABCABCABC they are bbb and ccc. So qb=bc\frac{q}{b} = \frac{b}{c}bq​=cb​, i.e. b2=cqb^2 = cqb2=cq.

    Add the two: a2+b2=cp+cq=c (p+q)=c⋅c=c2.■a^2 + b^2 = cp + cq = c\,(p + q) = c \cdot c = c^2. \qquad \blacksquarea2+b2=cp+cq=c(p+q)=c⋅c=c2.■

    No areas and no rearranging: once the similarity is set up, the theorem is two cross-multiplications and a factorisation.

  7. 7The algebra underneath: vectors

    For vectors, ∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+2 u⋅v+∣v∣2|u + v|^2 = |u|^2 + 2\,u \cdot v + |v|^2∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+2u⋅v+∣v∣2. Perpendicular means u⋅v=0u \cdot v = 0u⋅v=0, and then ∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+∣v∣2|u + v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+∣v∣2.

    Expand (u+v)⋅(u+v)(u + v) \cdot (u + v)(u+v)⋅(u+v) exactly like (x+y)2(x + y)^2(x+y)2: the dot product distributes over addition and is symmetric. With the legs as u,vu, vu,v the hypotenuse is u+vu + vu+v, and the cross term vanishes because the legs are perpendicular.

    More detail

    Take the legs as vectors uuu (length aaa) and vvv (length bbb), head to tail, so the hypotenuse is u+vu + vu+v (length ccc). Using only that the dot product distributes and is symmetric: c2=(u+v)⋅(u+v)=u⋅u+2 u⋅v+v⋅v=a2+2 u⋅v+b2.c^2 = (u + v) \cdot (u + v) = u \cdot u + 2\,u \cdot v + v \cdot v = a^2 + 2\,u \cdot v + b^2.c2=(u+v)⋅(u+v)=u⋅u+2u⋅v+v⋅v=a2+2u⋅v+b2. The legs are perpendicular, so u⋅v=0u \cdot v = 0u⋅v=0 and c2=a2+b2c^2 = a^2 + b^2c2=a2+b2.

    What this does and doesn't prove: in coordinates ∣u∣2=u12+u22|u|^2 = u_1^2 + u_2^2∣u∣2=u12​+u22​ is itself Pythagoras along the axes, so in the plane this is not an independent proof. What it shows is the structure. The theorem is exactly the cross term 2 u⋅v2\,u \cdot v2u⋅v vanishing, so it holds wherever lengths come from a dot product: in Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn (a box's space diagonal has d2=x2+y2+z2d^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2d2=x2+y2+z2), and for functions, where it becomes Parseval's identity in Fourier analysis.

    If the angle between the sides is γ\gammaγ instead of 90∘90^\circ90∘, the head-to-tail vectors make an angle 180∘−γ180^\circ - \gamma180∘−γ, so u⋅v=−abcos⁡γu \cdot v = -ab\cos\gammau⋅v=−abcosγ and the same expansion gives the law of cosines c2=a2+b2−2abcos⁡γc^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gammac2=a2+b2−2abcosγ.

  8. 8What the proofs used

    The first two proofs rely on the angle sum 180∘180^\circ180∘ — i.e. on Euclid's parallel postulate.

    On a sphere, triangle angles sum to more than 180∘180^\circ180∘ and the theorem fails.

    More detail

    Take the triangle on the Earth with one corner at the North Pole and two on the equator, a quarter-turn apart. All three angles are 90∘90^\circ90∘, and all three sides have the same length, so a2+b2=2c2≠c2a^2 + b^2 = 2c^2 \ne c^2a2+b2=2c2=c2.

    In the vector version the same assumption is hidden in the dot product: flat (Euclidean) space is exactly the setting where lengths come from one. The converse also holds: if a2+b2=c2a^2 + b^2 = c^2a2+b2=c2, the angle opposite ccc is a right angle (set cos⁡γ=0\cos\gamma = 0cosγ=0 in the law of cosines).

Takeaway. Compute one quantity in two ways and equate them: an area (first proof), a ratio (second). Underneath, the theorem says a cross term vanishes for perpendicular vectors.
