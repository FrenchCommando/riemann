Viscosity smooths: the heat equation

Joseph Fourier, Théorie analytique de la chaleur, 1822
Theorem

Let fff be 2π2\pi2π-periodic and integrable, with Fourier coefficients ckc_kck​. Then u(x,t)=∑k∈Zck e−νk2t eikxu(x, t) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb Z} c_k\, e^{-\nu k^2 t}\, e^{ikx}u(x,t)=k∈Z∑​ck​e−νk2teikx solves ∂tu=ν ∂x2u\partial_t u = \nu\,\partial_x^2 u∂t​u=ν∂x2​u with u(⋅,0)=fu(\cdot, 0) = fu(⋅,0)=f, and for every t>0t \gt 0t>0 it is infinitely differentiable, even if fff has jumps.

The term νΔu\nu \Delta uνΔu in the Navier–Stokes equations is the heat equation's diffusion. Before facing the nonlinear fluid equations, see exactly what diffusion does on its own: it kills each Fourier wave at a rate νk2\nu k^2νk2, so the finest details vanish first, and any rough initial state becomes smooth at once.

  5. 5Small scales smooth fastest

    If u(x,t)u(x, t)u(x,t) solves the heat equation, so does u(λx,λ2t)u(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t)u(λx,λ2t): a pattern λ\lambdaλ times smaller smooths λ2\lambda^2λ2 times faster.

    This is the same scaling as Navier–Stokes, x→λxx \to \lambda xx→λx, t→λ2tt \to \lambda^2 tt→λ2t. It's why viscosity is the hope for smoothness: at small scales it acts fastest.

    The catch, which the energy identity's last step quantifies: in Navier–Stokes the nonlinear term scales exactly like the viscous one, so zooming in doesn't let viscosity win automatically. Whether it wins anyway is the Millennium question.

Takeaway. Diffusion damps each wave at rate νk2\nu k^2νk2: rough data become smooth instantly, energy decreases, and small scales are erased first.
