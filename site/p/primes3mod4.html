Infinitely many primes ≡3(mod4)\equiv 3 \pmod 4≡3(mod4)

A variation on Euclid; general case Dirichlet, 1837
Theorem

There are infinitely many primes of the form 4k+34k + 34k+3.

Euclid's trick, adapted to an arithmetic progression. Seeing why it works for 4k+34k+34k+3 but not 4k+14k+14k+1 is the lesson.

Proof · 5 steps
  1. 1Odd primes come in two kinds

    Every odd prime is ≡1\equiv 1≡1 or ≡3(mod4)\equiv 3 \pmod 4≡3(mod4).

    An odd number leaves remainder 1 or 3 on division by 4 (0 and 2 are even).

    Examples: 5,13,17,29≡15, 13, 17, 29 \equiv 15,13,17,29≡1; 3,7,11,19,23≡3(mod4)3, 7, 11, 19, 23 \equiv 3 \pmod 43,7,11,19,23≡3(mod4).

  2. 2Ones are closed under multiplication

    A product of numbers ≡1(mod4)\equiv 1 \pmod 4≡1(mod4) is ≡1(mod4)\equiv 1 \pmod 4≡1(mod4).

    Congruences multiply: 1⋅1⋅…⋅1≡11 \cdot 1 \cdot \ldots \cdot 1 \equiv 11⋅1⋅…⋅1≡1.

    More detail

    So a number ≡3(mod4)\equiv 3 \pmod 4≡3(mod4) cannot have all its prime factors ≡1\equiv 1≡1. If it is odd, at least one of its prime factors is ≡3\equiv 3≡3.

    The reverse is false: 3⋅7=21≡13 \cdot 7 = 21 \equiv 13⋅7=21≡1. A number ≡1\equiv 1≡1 can be built entirely from 3s — which is why this proof doesn't adapt to 4k+14k+14k+1.
