On the way to Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness

Navier–Stokes: energy only goes down, and why that isn't enough

Jean Leray, 1934; the starting point of every Navier–Stokes theory
Theorem

A smooth, periodic, incompressible solution of the Navier–Stokes equations with viscosity ν>0\nu \gt 0ν>0 and no forcing satisfies ddt 12∫∣u∣2 dx  =  −ν∫∣∇u∣2 dx  ≤  0.\frac{d}{dt}\, \frac12\int |u|^2\,dx \;=\; -\nu \int |\nabla u|^2\,dx \;\le\; 0.dtd​21​∫∣u∣2dx=−ν∫∣∇u∣2dx≤0. But the energy is supercritical: under the equation's scaling symmetry it shrinks at small scales, so it cannot rule out blow-up.

The Navier–Stokes equations describe a viscous, incompressible fluid. The Millennium problem asks whether smooth solutions in 3D stay smooth forever. This proof gives the one bound everyone has, the energy identity, and then shows by a scaling argument why it falls short: that gap is the whole difficulty.

Builds on The Pythagorean theorem, Viscosity smooths: the heat equation.

Takeaway. Conservation laws give bounds; scaling tells you whether those bounds can control small scales. For 3D Navier–Stokes, the energy bound loses to scaling by one power of λ\lambdaλ.
