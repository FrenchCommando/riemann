On the way to The Riemann Hypothesis

Leonhard Euler, 1737
Theorem

∑p prime1p=12+13+15+17+111+⋯=∞.\sum_{p \text{ prime}} \frac1p = \frac12 + \frac13 + \frac15 + \frac17 + \frac1{11} + \cdots = \infty.p prime∑​p1​=21​+31​+51​+71​+111​+⋯=∞.

Euclid shows the primes never stop; Euler shows they are dense — denser than the squares, since ∑1/n2\sum 1/n^2∑1/n2 converges. The tool is the Euler product, which links primes to ζ(s)=∑n−s\zeta(s) = \sum n^{-s}ζ(s)=∑n−s and is the doorway to Riemann's 1859 paper.

Builds on Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, There are infinitely many primes.

    At s=1s = 1s=1 the product diverges — which is exactly what we just exploited. For s>1s \gt 1s>1 both sides converge and are equal; uniqueness of factorisation is what makes it an identity rather than an inequality.

    Riemann (1859) extended ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) to complex sss and showed the distribution of primes is controlled by its zeros. The Riemann Hypothesis — all non-trivial zeros have real part 12\tfrac1221​ — is equivalent to the sharpest form of the Prime Number Theorem's error term.

Takeaway. Encode primes in a product, unique factorisation turns it into a sum over all integers, and analysis (log, series bounds) pulls information back about the primes.
