Fermat's Last Theorem

Stated by Fermat c. 1637; proved by Andrew Wiles, with Richard Taylor, 1995
Theorem

For every n≥3n \ge 3n≥3 there are no positive integers x,y,zx, y, zx,y,z with xn+yn=znx^n + y^n = z^nxn+yn=zn.

The full proof runs to hundreds of pages of modern algebraic geometry, so this is a map rather than a walkthrough: each step either computes something you can check by hand or states a major theorem with its source. The route: turn a hypothetical solution into an elliptic curve so strange that it can't be "modular", then prove that every such curve is modular.

Roadmap, not a full proof. Steps marked cited state a major result with its source instead of proving it; the rest are worked out here.

  1. 1What is left to provecited

    It suffices to show xp+yp=zpx^p + y^p = z^pxp+yp=zp has no positive solutions for every prime p≥5p \ge 5p≥5.

    Fermat (n=4n = 4n=4, previous proof); Euler, 1770 (p=3p = 3p=3)

    The n=4n = 4n=4 proof reduced everything to odd primes, and p=3p = 3p=3 was settled by Euler (with a gap later filled). From now on p≥5p \ge 5p≥5 is prime and, for contradiction, ap+bp=cpa^p + b^p = c^pap+bp=cp with a,b,ca, b, ca,b,c positive and pairwise coprime.

    More detail

    Dividing out common factors, a solution can be assumed primitive: gcd⁡(a,b,c)=1\gcd(a, b, c) = 1gcd(a,b,c)=1, and then pairwise coprime (a prime dividing two of them divides the third ppp-th power, hence the third number). Exactly one of a,b,ca, b, ca,b,c is even, as with Pythagorean triples. By rearranging (and replacing a term by its negative, allowed since ppp is odd) one can arrange bbb even and a≡−1(mod4)a \equiv -1 \pmod 4a≡−1(mod4). That normalisation is what makes the next steps' curve as nice as possible.

  6. 6Ribet: Frey's curve can't be modularcited

    If the Frey curve were modular, it would correspond to a weight-2 modular form of level 2. There are none. So it is not modular.

    Ribet, 1990 (announced 1986), proving Serre's epsilon conjecture

    Ribet's level-lowering theorem: when the mod-ppp representation of a modular curve is unramified at a prime ℓ\ellℓ, the matching modular form can be replaced by one of level with ℓ\ellℓ removed. For the Frey curve that strips every odd prime of abcabcabc, leaving level 2, and the space of weight-2 cusp forms of level 2 is zero.

    More detail

    Every step here is a deep theorem, so take this one as a black box. The shape of the argument: modularity gives a form fff of level N=∏ℓ∣abcℓN = \prod_{\ell \mid abc} \ellN=∏ℓ∣abc​ℓ (roughly). The ppp-th power discriminant makes the Galois representation on the curve's ppp-torsion points unramified at each odd ℓ∣abc\ell \mid abcℓ∣abc. Ribet shows that then a form of level N/ℓN/\ellN/ℓ also matches mod ppp; repeat until the level is 222. Since dim⁡S2(Γ0(2))=0\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(2)) = 0dimS2​(Γ0​(2))=0, there is nothing to match: contradiction. So modularity of semistable curves implies Fermat.

  7. 7Wiles: semistable curves are modularcited

    Every semistable elliptic curve over Q\mathbb{Q}Q is modular. Hence the Frey curve can't exist, and Fermat's Last Theorem is true. ■\qquad\blacksquare■

    Wiles, Annals of Mathematics 1995; Taylor–Wiles, same issue (fixing a gap found in 1993)

    Wiles proved a modularity lifting theorem: if a curve's mod-333 representation is modular (known from Langlands–Tunnell), so is the curve, with a "3–5 switch" when the mod-3 picture degenerates. Taylor–Wiles supplied the key step after the 1993 announcement was found to have a gap.

    More detail

    The heart is an "R=TR = TR=T" theorem: a ring RRR parametrising all ways of deforming a Galois representation equals a Hecke ring TTT built from modular forms. Equality means every deformation, in particular the one coming from the curve, is modular.

    Afterwards: Breuil, Conrad, Diamond and Taylor (2001) removed "semistable": every elliptic curve over Q\mathbb{Q}Q is modular. The same circle of ideas, the Langlands program, is what reporting on the 2026 Riemann Hypothesis proof says it draws on.

Takeaway. A Diophantine equation was solved by translating a solution into an object (an elliptic curve) and proving that object is too strange to exist. The bridge, modularity, is a special case of the Langlands program.
