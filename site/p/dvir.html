On the way to The Kakeya conjecture

Kakeya sets over finite fields

Zeev Dvir, 2008; the question: Thomas Wolff, 1999
Theorem

Let K⊆FqnK \subseteq \mathbb F_q^nK⊆Fqn​ contain a line in every direction. Then ∣K∣≥(q+n−1n)≥qnn!.|K| \ge \binom{q + n - 1}{n} \ge \frac{q^n}{n!}.∣K∣≥(nq+n−1​)≥n!qn​. So a Kakeya set over a finite field fills a fixed fraction of the whole space.

Wolff proposed the Kakeya problem over a finite field as a toy model of the real one: no tubes, no δ\deltaδ, just lines of qqq points. Experts expected it to be nearly as hard. Dvir solved it in a page, with the polynomial method: if KKK were small, some low-degree polynomial would vanish on it, and vanishing on a line in every direction is too much for a low-degree polynomial. The argument doesn't transfer to the real numbers, but it changed how people attack the real problem.

    More detail

    In the plane the bound is (q+12)=q(q+1)/2\binom{q+1}{2} = q(q+1)/2(2q+1​)=q(q+1)/2, about half the plane, and the figure's construction has q(q+1)/2+(q−1)/2q(q+1)/2 + (q - 1)/2q(q+1)/2+(q−1)/2 points, so for n=2n = 2n=2 the bound is nearly exact.

    In higher dimensions 1/n!1/n!1/n! is far from the truth. Dvir, Kopparty, Saraf and Sudan (2009) improved it to about qn/2nq^n/2^nqn/2n by asking PPP to vanish to high order at each point of KKK, the "method of multiplicities".

    Over R\mathbb RR the argument has nothing to grip: real lines have infinitely many points, and the real problem is about δ\deltaδ-tubes, which a polynomial can't "vanish on". What carried over is the idea that the hard examples sit near low-degree algebraic surfaces, which became central to the 3D work.

Takeaway. A small set lies in the zero set of a low-degree polynomial, and a low-degree polynomial can't vanish on a line in every direction. So Kakeya sets over Fq\mathbb F_qFq​ have at least qn/n!q^n/n!qn/n! points.
