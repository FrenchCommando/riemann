The Kakeya conjecture

Sōichi Kakeya, 1917; the conjecture in n dimensions, from Davies 1971; proved in 3D by Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl, 2025
Theorem

A set in Rn\mathbb R^nRn that contains a unit segment in every direction has Minkowski and Hausdorff dimension nnn. Proved for n=2n = 2n=2 (Davies, 1971) and n=3n = 3n=3 (Wang and Zahl, 2025).

The path here built three things: Kakeya sets of area as small as you like (so area is the wrong measure), Córdoba's argument that in the plane they are still two-dimensional, and Dvir's proof that over finite fields they fill a fixed fraction of space. In three dimensions Córdoba's argument stops at dimension 222, and the finite-field proof has nothing to hold on to. This roadmap covers why harmonic analysts care, the bounds between 1991 and 2019, the 2025 proof by Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl, and what is still open.

Roadmap, not a full proof. Steps marked cited state a major result with its source instead of proving it; the rest are worked out here.

  1. 1The conjecture, preciselycited

    A Kakeya set in Rn\mathbb R^nRn is a compact set containing a unit segment in every direction. The conjecture says its δ\deltaδ-neighbourhood has volume at least cεδεc_\varepsilon \delta^{\varepsilon}cε​δε for every ε>0\varepsilon \gt 0ε>0 (Minkowski dimension nnn), and its Hausdorff dimension is nnn too.

    Davies, Math. Proc. Cambridge Philos. Soc. 69 (1971), for n=2n = 2n=2

    Besicovitch's construction works in every dimension (take a planar Kakeya set times a cube), so Kakeya sets of volume zero exist for n≥2n \ge 2n≥2. Dimension is the next-finer measure. In the plane, Córdoba's argument gives the Minkowski version with the loss 1/log⁡(1/δ)1/\log(1/\delta)1/log(1/δ), which is smaller than any δε\delta^{\varepsilon}δε.

    More detail

    Hausdorff dimension nnn implies Minkowski dimension nnn, since Minkowski dimension is never smaller, so the Hausdorff statement is the stronger one. In the plane both were known by 1977.

    For n=3n = 3n=3 the conjecture says: covering a Kakeya set by balls of radius δ\deltaδ takes about δ−3\delta^{-3}δ−3 balls, up to factors δ−ε\delta^{-\varepsilon}δ−ε. So a set of volume zero must be as "thick" as a solid cube at every resolution.

  2. 2Tubes are wavescited

    A wave whose frequencies point in nearly one direction travels along a tube. So Kakeya sets control how waves can pile up, and the restriction, Bochner–Riesz and local smoothing conjectures of harmonic analysis each imply the Kakeya conjecture.

    Fefferman, The multiplier problem for the ball, Ann. of Math. 94 (1971); Bourgain, 1991; Wolff's survey, 1999

    Fefferman used Besicovitch sets to show that cutting off a Fourier integral to a ball does not converge in LpL^pLp for p≠2p \ne 2p=2 in dimension ≥2\ge 2≥2: wave packets along the tubes of a Kakeya set of tiny volume can be arranged to pile up. Any small Kakeya set would give a similar counterexample to the restriction conjecture.

    More detail

    The idea is the uncertainty principle. A function whose Fourier transform lives on a cap of radius δ\deltaδ of the unit sphere, around the direction eee, is concentrated (over a region of size δ−2\delta^{-2}δ−2) on a tube of width δ−1\delta^{-1}δ−1 and length δ−2\delta^{-2}δ−2 in direction eee. Shrunk by the factor δ2\delta^2δ2, that is a tube of width δ\deltaδ and length 111. Sums of such packets over many caps are sums over tubes in many directions, and the question how much they can concentrate becomes the question how small a union of tubes in different directions can be.

    So Kakeya is the "geometric core" of these conjectures: necessary for each of them, not sufficient. Proving Kakeya in R3\mathbb R^3R3 does not prove restriction in R3\mathbb R^3R3.

  3. 3Bushes and hairbrushescited

    In R3\mathbb R^3R3 the dimension is at least 222 (Córdoba's argument, or the bush), 7/37/37/3 (Bourgain, 1991), 5/25/25/2 (Wolff's hairbrush, 1995), and slightly more than 5/25/25/2: Minkowski (Katz, Łaba and Tao, 2000) and Hausdorff (Katz and Zahl, 2019).

    Bourgain, GAFA 1991; Wolff, Rev. Mat. Iberoam. 11 (1995); Katz–Łaba–Tao, Ann. of Math. 152 (2000); Katz–Zahl, J. Amer. Math. Soc. 32 (2019)

    A bush is many tubes through one point: away from it they separate, so a point of high multiplicity forces a large union, which gives (n+1)/2(n + 1)/2(n+1)/2 in Rn\mathbb R^nRn. A hairbrush is many tubes crossing one tube, the stem: around the stem they spread over different planes, and each plane is a 2D Kakeya problem, where Córdoba's argument applies. Wolff's count gives (n+2)/2(n + 2)/2(n+2)/2 in Rn\mathbb R^nRn.

    More detail

    Every argument has the same shape as Córdoba's: count incidences between tubes, use that tubes in different directions meet in small sets, and turn little overlap into a large union. What changes is which configuration is counted: pairs (Córdoba), tubes through a point (bush), tubes through a tube (hairbrush).

    Wolff's 5/25/25/2 resisted for two decades. Katz, Łaba and Tao pushed the Minkowski dimension past it by an explicit tiny amount, by showing that a set of dimension near 5/25/25/2 would have to be very structured, and the structure is impossible.

  4. 4Where the hard cases hidecited

    A Kakeya set in R3\mathbb R^3R3 of dimension near 5/25/25/2 would have to be plany (the tubes through a typical point lie close to one plane) and grainy (at small scales it is made of flat slabs, grains).

    Katz–Łaba–Tao, 2000; Guth, the polynomial method, 2010s; Tao's account of Wang–Zahl, 2025

    These are the configurations every known argument fails to rule out: tubes lying in planes look like a lower-dimensional problem repeated, and the counting in bushes and hairbrushes cannot see that they are spread out. Guth later explained graininess with the polynomial method, the descendant of Dvir's finite-field proof.

    More detail

    This is where Dvir's idea came back to the real problem. Over Fq\mathbb F_qFq​ a small Kakeya set lies in the zero set of a low-degree polynomial. Over R\mathbb RR that is false, but a weakened version holds: the hard configurations concentrate near low-degree surfaces, and polynomial partitioning (Guth, Katz) cuts space into cells where the problem splits.

    A third property matters for the 2025 proof: a Kakeya set is sticky if tubes whose directions are close are also close in position, at every scale. Sticky sets look the same at every scale, which makes them good candidates for counterexamples.

  5. 5Wang and Zahl, 2025cited

    Every Kakeya set in R3\mathbb R^3R3 has Minkowski and Hausdorff dimension 333. Wang and Zahl first proved the sticky case (2022), then the general case from a stronger theorem: a union of δ\deltaδ-tubes, not too many of which lie in any one convex set, has almost maximal volume.

    Wang–Zahl, Sticky Kakeya sets and the sticky Kakeya conjecture, arXiv:2210.09581, J. Amer. Math. Soc. 39 (2026); Wang–Zahl, Volume estimates for unions of convex sets, and the Kakeya set conjecture in three dimensions, arXiv:2502.17655 (2025, 127 pages)

    The proof is an induction on scales: if every such union has volume at least δ3−d\delta^{3 - d}δ3−d (dimension ddd), show it has dimension d+αd + \alphad+α, and repeat until d=3d = 3d=3. Separated directions don't survive the induction, so the hypothesis is the weaker "convex Wolff axioms" (no convex set holds too many tubes), which does.

    More detail

    From Tao's account of the proof:

    • Sticky case. Treated in the 2022 paper, following a strategy of Katz and Tao: in the self-similar case, a small gain at one scale repeats at every scale.
    • Non-sticky case. Tubes are grouped into fat tubes at an intermediate scale and the multiplicities compared. Near-extremal sets must be grainy, by Guth's polynomial-method argument.
    • Planar grains are handled by Córdoba's L2L^2L2 argument, the one in this path.
    • A structure theorem groups grains into convex prisms. Thick prisms violate the induction hypothesis; thin ones are handled by Córdoba's argument again.

    Tao called the paper a historic breakthrough, and Nets Katz called it a solution to the "holy grail" problem.

  6. 6Status, November 2026cited

    Hong Wang received a Fields Medal on 23 July 2026, in part for this work. In autumn 2026 OpenAI posted preprints claiming the four-dimensional case and Sogge's local smoothing conjecture, from which they derive Kakeya, in its maximal-function form, in every dimension. As of November no expert assessment of either has been reported.

    ICM 2026, Philadelphia; OpenAI, Every four-dimensional Kakeya set has full Hausdorff dimension (dated 24 Sep 2026) and Local smoothing for the Euclidean wave equation (dated 25 Oct 2026); Scientific American, 6 October 2026

    The three-dimensional proof is accepted by experts, and its sticky half is published. The local smoothing preprint is 59 pages; OpenAI's catalogue lists its main theorems, including that every Kakeya set has full Hausdorff dimension, as formalized in Lean. The four-dimensional preprint, 175 pages, has no Lean entry of its own. OpenAI calls the collection raw research data and warns that its unformalized results could have issues.

    More detail

    What each claim says, from the preprints themselves:

    • n=4n = 4n=4 (175 pages): every subset of R4\mathbb R^4R4 containing a unit segment in every direction has Hausdorff dimension 444, with no compactness assumption. It builds on the three-dimensional theorem (through a later streamlined proof by Guth, Wang and Zahl), and its new device is local quadratic fits: line pieces that stay close to the zero set of one quadratic polynomial across many scales.
    • All n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 (59 pages): Sogge's local smoothing estimate for the wave equation, with the Bochner–Riesz conjecture, Fourier restriction for the sphere inside the conjectured range, and the Kakeya and Nikodym maximal conjectures derived from it. That would also settle the maximal-function form in R3\mathbb R^3R3, which Tao described as open in 2025.
    • Verification: a Lean proof shows that the formal statement follows; whether the formal statement says what the conjecture says is what reviewers check, as with the Navier–Stokes claim.

    If the local smoothing claim holds, it settles the Kakeya conjecture and much of the harmonic analysis in step 2 at once.

    As with the Riemann Hypothesis and Navier–Stokes entries: this is taken from the reporting listed below, written with Claude, and the situation is changing.

Takeaway. Kakeya sets can have volume zero but, conjecturally, never lower dimension. Tubes in different directions can't overlap much; in the plane Cauchy–Schwarz shows it, and in space it took a 127-page induction on scales, completed in 2025.
