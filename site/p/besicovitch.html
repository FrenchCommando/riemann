On the way to The Kakeya conjecture

Kakeya sets of tiny area

Abram Besicovitch, 1919 and 1928; simplified by Oskar Perron, 1928, and Isaac Schoenberg, 1962
Theorem

For every ε>0\varepsilon \gt 0ε>0 there is a set in the plane with area less than ε\varepsilonε that contains a segment of length 111 in every direction.

In 1917 Sōichi Kakeya asked for the smallest area in which a needle of length 111 can be turned around completely. A disc of diameter 111 works (area π/4≈0.79\pi/4 \approx 0.79π/4≈0.79), and a three-cornered deltoid does better (π/8≈0.39\pi/8 \approx 0.39π/8≈0.39). The answer is that there is no smallest area at all. This proof builds the key object: a set of area as small as you like that contains the needle in every direction. The trick is to cut a triangle into slivers and slide them over each other.

Builds on The Pythagorean theorem.

Proof · 5 steps
  1. 1Triangles hold directions

    An equilateral triangle TTT of height 111 contains a unit segment in every direction within 30∘30^\circ30∘ of its axis. Three such triangles, turned by 60∘60^\circ60∘ from each other, cover all directions.

    From the apex, the segment to a point of the base has length between 111 (straight down) and 2/32/\sqrt32/3​ (to a corner), and its direction sweeps the 60∘60^\circ60∘ apex angle. Three sweeps of 60∘60^\circ60∘ make 180∘180^\circ180∘, which is every direction of a line.

    More detail

    A segment has a direction "up to sign": turning it by 180∘180^\circ180∘ gives the same segment. So covering 180∘180^\circ180∘ of directions is enough.

    From here on, only translations are allowed. If TTT is cut into pieces and each piece is moved without turning, each piece still holds its segments, in the same directions. So the goal becomes: cut TTT into pieces and translate them so that they overlap almost entirely, while their union still contains a translate of every piece.

  5. 5Area zero, and turning the needlecited

    There are Kakeya sets of area exactly zero, and a unit needle can be turned all the way around inside a set of area less than any ε>0\varepsilon \gt 0ε>0.

    Besicovitch, 1919 and Math. Z. 27 (1928); Pál, 1921; Falconer, The Geometry of Fractal Sets, 1985, ch. 7

    Area zero: Besicovitch used a limiting version of the construction, each stage inside a small neighbourhood of the previous one, with a compactness argument keeping a unit segment in every direction in the limit. Turning: a needle can move between two parallel positions using extra area as small as you like (Pál's joins), so the needle turns a little inside one sliver, jumps to the next sliver, and so on.

    More detail

    Pál's join. To get from a line ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ to a parallel line ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​: slide far along ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​, rotate slightly onto a line crossing ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ far away, slide back along that line, and rotate onto ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​. The two rotations sweep thin triangles whose area shrinks the further you travel. So Kakeya's question has no answer: no positive area is the smallest.

    What survives is dimension. These sets have area zero, but the next proof shows that in the plane they are still two-dimensional: covering one by discs of radius δ\deltaδ takes almost as many discs as covering a whole square.

Takeaway. Cut a triangle into slivers and slide them over each other, and the union shrinks as much as you like while keeping every direction. Kakeya sets can have area as small as you like, even zero.
