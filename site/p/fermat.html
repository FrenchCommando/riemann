Fermat's Little Theorem

Stated by Fermat, 1640; proved by Euler, 1736
Theorem

If ppp is prime, then ap≡a(modp)a^p \equiv a \pmod pap≡a(modp) for every integer aaa. If moreover p∤ap \nmid ap∤a, then ap−1≡1(modp)a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod pap−1≡1(modp).

The engine behind RSA and most primality tests. This proof is Euler's: binomial coefficients plus induction on aaa.

Builds on Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

    For 0<k<p0 \lt k \lt p0<k<p,  p∣(pk)\ p \mid \binom pk p∣(kp​).

    Write p!=(pk)⋅k!⋅(p−k)!p! = \binom pk \cdot k! \cdot (p-k)!p!=(kp​)⋅k!⋅(p−k)!.

    ppp divides the left side. On the right, k!k!k! and (p−k)!(p-k)!(p−k)! are products of numbers 1,2,…1, 2, \dots1,2,…, all less than ppp when 0<k<p0 \lt k \lt p0<k<p. A prime can't divide a number between 1 and p−1p-1p−1, so by Euclid's lemma ppp doesn't divide k! (p−k)!k!\,(p-k)!k!(p−k)!.

  3. 3Induction on a ≥ 0

    ap≡a(modp)a^p \equiv a \pmod pap≡a(modp) for all a≥0a \ge 0a≥0.

    Base: 0p=00^p = 00p=0. Step: if ap≡aa^p \equiv aap≡a, then (a+1)p≡ap+1≡a+1(a+1)^p \equiv a^p + 1 \equiv a + 1(a+1)p≡ap+1≡a+1.

    More detail

    Let P(a)P(a)P(a) be the statement ap≡a(modp)a^p \equiv a \pmod pap≡a(modp).

    • P(0)P(0)P(0): 0p=00^p = 00p=0. ✓
    • P(a)⇒P(a+1)P(a) \Rightarrow P(a+1)P(a)⇒P(a+1): by the previous step, (a+1)p≡ap+1(a+1)^p \equiv a^p + 1(a+1)p≡ap+1, and by P(a)P(a)P(a) this is ≡a+1\equiv a + 1≡a+1. ✓

    Intuition: raising to the ppp-th power commutes with "adding 1" mod ppp, and every non-negative aaa is 0+1+1+⋯+10 + 1 + 1 + \cdots + 10+1+1+⋯+1.

  4. 4Negative a

    The congruence holds for all integers aaa.

    Any aaa is congruent to some b∈{0,…,p−1}b \in \{0, \dots, p-1\}b∈{0,…,p−1}, and powers respect congruence: ap≡bp≡b≡aa^p \equiv b^p \equiv b \equiv aap≡bp≡b≡a.

    More detail

    Pick b≥0b \ge 0b≥0 with a≡b(modp)a \equiv b \pmod pa≡b(modp) (e.g. the remainder of aaa on division by ppp). Congruences multiply, so ap≡bpa^p \equiv b^pap≡bp. By the previous step bp≡bb^p \equiv bbp≡b, and b≡ab \equiv ab≡a.

  5. 5Divide by a

    If p∤ap \nmid ap∤a, then ap−1≡1(modp)a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod pap−1≡1(modp).

    a⋅ap−1≡a⋅1a \cdot a^{p-1} \equiv a \cdot 1a⋅ap−1≡a⋅1, and we may cancel aaa because gcd⁡(a,p)=1\gcd(a, p) = 1gcd(a,p)=1.

    More detail

    We know p∣ap−a=a(ap−1−1)p \mid a^p - a = a(a^{p-1} - 1)p∣ap−a=a(ap−1−1). By Euclid's lemma, p∣ap \mid ap∣a or p∣ap−1−1p \mid a^{p-1} - 1p∣ap−1−1. The first is excluded, so ap−1≡1a^{p-1} \equiv 1ap−1≡1.

    This is the form used in practice: e.g. the Fermat primality test picks aaa and checks whether an−1≡1(modn)a^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod nan−1≡1(modn). If not, nnn is certainly composite.

Takeaway. Mod a prime, x↦xpx \mapsto x^px↦xp respects addition; induction from 0 then forces it to be the identity.
