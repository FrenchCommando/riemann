On the way to Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness

3D is different: vortex stretching

Helmholtz, 1858 (vortex dynamics); Beale–Kato–Majda, 1984 (blow-up criterion)
Theorem

In 3D the vorticity ω=∇×u\omega = \nabla\times uω=∇×u obeys ∂tω+(u⋅∇)ω=(ω⋅∇)u+νΔω.\partial_t\omega + (u\cdot\nabla)\omega = (\omega\cdot\nabla)u + \nu\Delta\omega.∂t​ω+(u⋅∇)ω=(ω⋅∇)u+νΔω. The stretching term (ω⋅∇)u(\omega\cdot\nabla)u(ω⋅∇)u vanishes for 2D flows and can amplify vorticity in 3D. A smooth solution breaks down at a time T∗T^*T∗ only if ∫0T∗max⁡x∣ω∣ dt=∞\int_0^{T^*}\max_x|\omega|\,dt = \infty∫0T∗​maxx​∣ω∣dt=∞.

The 2D proof worked because vorticity could only be carried and diffused. In 3D one more term appears, and it's the one physicists see in every turbulent flow: vortex tubes stretched by the flow spin faster and thinner. This proof finds the term, shows exactly why 2D escapes it, and explains why it makes blow-up conceivable.

    Geometrically: in a planar flow the vortex lines point straight out of the plane, and nothing ever varies in that direction, so they can't be stretched. The 3D equation then reduces exactly to the 2D vorticity equation from the previous stop.

    In the straining flow u=α (−x12,−x22,x3)u = \alpha\,(-\tfrac{x_1}{2}, -\tfrac{x_2}{2}, x_3)u=α(−2x1​​,−2x2​​,x3​), vorticity along the x3x_3x3​-axis feels (ω⋅∇)u=α ω(\omega\cdot\nabla)u = \alpha\,\omega(ω⋅∇)u=αω: it grows like eαte^{\alpha t}eαt until diffusion stops it.

    So, following a fluid particle on the axis and ignoring diffusion, dω3dt=α ω3\frac{d\omega_3}{dt} = \alpha\,\omega_3dtdω3​​=αω3​: exponential growth.     More detail

    Why this model: the stretching term (ω⋅∇)u(\omega\cdot\nabla)u(ω⋅∇)u multiplies ω\omegaω by a velocity gradient, and velocity gradients are typically about as large as the vorticity itself (on average they are exactly as large: ∫∣∇u∣2=∫∣ω∣2\int|\nabla u|^2 = \int|\omega|^2∫∣∇u∣2=∫∣ω∣2 holds in 3D too, by the same integration by parts as in the previous proof). So, very roughly, ddt∣ω∣≲∣ω∣2\frac{d}{dt}|\omega| \lesssim |\omega|^2dtd​∣ω∣≲∣ω∣2: a growth rate that increases with the size, like y′=y2y' = y^2y′=y2, rather than a fixed rate like the strain example.

    This is only a caricature. The true equation has transport, diffusion and geometry (the stretching depends on how vortex lines are aligned with the strain), and they could prevent the blow-up. Whether they always do is the 3D question.

  5. 5Blow-up means vorticity blows upcited

    A smooth solution on [0,T∗)[0, T^*)[0,T∗) can be continued past T∗T^*T∗ unless ∫0T∗max⁡x∣ω(x,t)∣ dt=∞\displaystyle\int_0^{T^*}\max_x|\omega(x, t)|\,dt = \infty∫0T∗​xmax​∣ω(x,t)∣dt=∞.

    Beale, Kato and Majda, Comm. Math. Phys. 1984 (for Euler; the same holds for Navier–Stokes)

    So every possible singularity is a vorticity singularity: if the largest vorticity stays integrable in time, all higher derivatives stay bounded. In 2D the maximum of ∣ω∣|\omega|∣ω∣ can't increase at all (previous stop), so the criterion holds trivially.

    More detail

    This turns the Millennium question into a question about one quantity: can the stretching term drive max⁡∣ω∣\max|\omega|max∣ω∣ to infinity fast enough? Refinements say the direction of vorticity matters too: if the vortex lines stay smoothly aligned where ∣ω∣|\omega|∣ω∣ is large, there is no blow-up (Constantin and Fefferman, 1993). A blow-up would need vortex lines to twist sharply as they intensify.

    That is where the search for a singularity starts: the roadmap at the end of this path picks up from here.

Takeaway. 3D vorticity can be stretched, and stretching makes it grow faster the larger it is. Any blow-up must be a vorticity blow-up, which is where the Millennium problem lives.
