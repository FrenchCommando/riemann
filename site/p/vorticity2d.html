On the way to Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness

2D flows stay smooth: the vorticity bound

Ladyzhenskaya, 1959 (Navier–Stokes); Wolibner 1933 and Yudovich 1963 (Euler)
Theorem

For the 2D Navier–Stokes equations on the torus, the vorticity ω=∂1u2−∂2u1\omega = \partial_1 u_2 - \partial_2 u_1ω=∂1​u2​−∂2​u1​ satisfies ddt 12∫ω2  =  −ν∫∣∇ω∣2  ≤  0,\frac{d}{dt}\,\frac12\int \omega^2 \;=\; -\nu\int |\nabla\omega|^2 \;\le\; 0,dtd​21​∫ω2=−ν∫∣∇ω∣2≤0, and ∫∣∇u∣2=∫ω2\int |\nabla u|^2 = \int \omega^2∫∣∇u∣2=∫ω2. So the size of the velocity gradients never exceeds its initial value, which rules out blow-up in 2D.

The energy identity bounds ∫∣u∣2\int |u|^2∫∣u∣2, but that is too weak to stop small scales from forming. In 2D there is a second bound, one derivative stronger: the vorticity can't grow. That extra derivative is exactly what scaling demanded, and it's why the 2D problem was solved in 1959 while 3D is a Millennium problem.

  5. 5Why this beats scaling in 2Dcited

    Under the zoom u↦λu(λx,λ2t)u \mapsto \lambda u(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t)u↦λu(λx,λ2t), the 2D enstrophy is multiplied by λ2\lambda^2λ2. A solution concentrating at small scales would need unbounded enstrophy, but enstrophy is bounded by its initial value. With this bound, solutions stay smooth for all time.

    Ladyzhenskaya, 1959, for the bootstrap from the enstrophy bound to full smoothness

    ∣∇uλ∣2|\nabla u_\lambda|^2∣∇uλ​∣2 picks up λ4\lambda^4λ4 (one λ\lambdaλ from the amplitude and one from the derivative, squared), and the area element λ−2\lambda^{-2}λ−2: net λ2\lambda^2λ2. Zooming in makes enstrophy larger, so a bounded enstrophy forbids zooming in indefinitely.

    More detail

    This is the gap the energy proof identified, closed. Energy, which scales like λ−1\lambda^{-1}λ−1 in 3D and λ0\lambda^0λ0 in 2D, was not strong enough to stop concentration in 3D. In 2D the enstrophy bound is a full derivative stronger, and turning it into bounds on every higher derivative is a routine (if lengthy) bootstrap, cited here.

    What makes 2D special is the vorticity equation itself: vorticity is only transported and diffused. In 3D, vorticity is a vector, and its equation has one extra term, (ω⋅∇)u(\omega\cdot\nabla)u(ω⋅∇)u, which can create vorticity. That's the next stop.

Takeaway. In 2D vorticity is only carried and diffused, so it can't grow; its size controls every velocity gradient, which beats scaling and keeps flows smooth.

Used by 3D is different: vortex stretching.
